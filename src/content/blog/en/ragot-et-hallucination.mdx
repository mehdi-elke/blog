You’ve spent weeks refining your **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)** system. It’s fast, it’s sleek, but once you push it a bit, it starts spreading "RAG-tags" and tall tales. A made-up date here, an off-topic answer there...

The problem? Evaluating a generative AI system by hand is a massive, costly, and non-reproducible task.

It’s time to open court to ensure the reliability of your deployments. Let's appoint a new kind of magistrate: **the LLM-as-a-Judge.**

## The 4 Counts of Indictment

In a RAG system, you don’t judge an answer in isolation. We break it down into four precise points to know exactly which part—the retriever or the generator—is at fault.

<Image src={simplifiedRag} alt="Simplified RAG scheme" class="my-8 border border-border-main" />

### 1. The Sloppy Investigation (Context Precision)

Is the retriever fetching the right documents? If the judge finds too much "noise" in the provided evidence, the verdict is clear: your indexing needs work before blaming the AI.

### 2. Procedural Error (Context Recall)

Did we miss crucial evidence? If the information exists in your database but never reaches the LLM's desk, your system suffers from selective amnesia.

### 3. Perjury (Faithfulness / Groundedness)

This is pure hallucination. The chatbot claims things that are not in the documents. It "invents" evidence—like asserting the company was founded in 1990 when the source document clearly states 1995. This is the major risk in enterprise settings.

### 4. Contempt of Court (Answer Relevancy)

The chatbot isn't really answering the question. An answer can be technically true and faithful to the sources, but completely off-topic. It’s the expert syndrome: talking just to hear oneself speak.

## Is the Judge Impartial?

Trusting one LLM to evaluate another might seem counterintuitive. Yet, the task of "judging" is cognitively less complex than "generating." But beware: like any magistrate, our judge has its own biases:

- **Verbosity Bias:** It tends to rate "lawyers" who talk more highly, even if they're just padding.

- **Self-Preference Bias:** A GPT-4 judge will tend to prefer writing styles similar to its own.

- **Position Bias:** When comparing two answers, the one presented first is sometimes unfairly favored.

To avoid arbitrary sentencing, we force the judge to **justify its reasoning step-by-step** before rendering a verdict. By asking it to explain why it gave a score of 0.8, we drastically reduce judgmental errors and make the evaluation transparent.

## Deliberation: Don't Change the Culprit Too Quickly

The first reflex when facing a bad answer is to change the model (switching from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4o, for example). This can be a strategic error.

I have found several times that the hidden culprit was the retrieval. Improving chunk quality, refining metadata, or adding a reranking step is often more effective and cheaper than inflating the power of the final brain.
